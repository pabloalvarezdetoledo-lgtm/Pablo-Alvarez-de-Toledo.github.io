The data published by *El País* on December 2, 2025, reads, at first glance, like a triumph: unemployment fell by 19,000 people in November, and job destruction was halved compared with the same month a year earlier. Headlines of this kind invite optimism, and understandably so — after more than a decade of the Spanish labor market serving as a byword for European dysfunction, a period of falling unemployment and slowing job losses feels like vindication. But a rigorous economic reading has to peel back the aggregate figures and ask a harder question: are we watching the productive structure of the Spanish economy genuinely strengthen, or merely the upward swing of a labor market that has always been unusually volatile? The Spanish economy has historically exhibited far greater employment volatility than its European neighbors, and that volatility cuts both ways. Good employment numbers during a growth phase are not necessarily evidence of successful policy; they are often the mechanical consequence of a production model that is labor-abundant rather than capital-intensive.

That distinction is the key to reading the November figures correctly. The headline numbers — 21.82 million people in registered employment, and registered unemployment down to 2.42 million — need to be interpreted through the lens of Spain's factor endowment. The Spanish productive fabric relies on human capital, rather than capital deployment, to make marginal adjustments to output. Compare this with a capital-intensive economy such as Germany's: when a German downturn hits, firms are more likely to idle a few machines and lay off a handful of the operators who ran them. In Spain, the same shock is absorbed disproportionately by labor — instead of switching off a machine, firms let ten workers go. The relationship is symmetric in the upswing, too. In a recovery like the current one, Spain creates jobs faster than its GDP grows precisely because firms are "turning on workers" rather than deepening their capital stock. The resilience seen in November 2025 is genuinely good news for the households it touches, but it also exposes a structural vulnerability: a recovery driven by adding a variable factor, labor, rather than by deepening the capital structure — machinery, technology, process — is not the same thing as a more robust economy. We are hiring more; it does not follow that we are building something sturdier, rather than simply re-inflating a labor-intensive layer of production that will be the first to evaporate the moment the cycle turns.

This has direct implications for the debate over fiscal policy. It is common to hear that reducing deficits is self-defeating if it chokes off growth, and that markets ultimately reward growth above all else. That is true as far as it goes — but growth is not homogeneous, and its source matters as much as its rate. There is a meaningful difference between organic growth, financed by genuine savings and private investment, and growth manufactured by public deficit spending. The lesson of past crises was probably not that balanced budgets are themselves the enemy, but that the wrong method was sometimes used to reach them — raising taxes on the private sector to sustain a bloated public structure, a form of "austerity" that erodes the very tax base it is meant to protect. Properly understood, though, maintaining balanced public accounts is not an accounting nicety; it is a precondition for sustainable private-sector growth. When government runs a chronic deficit, it draws down the finite pool of national savings, crowding out private investment and distorting the structure of production. The genuine reassurance markets need, and the genuine protection the middle class needs, comes from a solvent state that is not mortgaging future tax revenue to subsidize today's consumption. Fiscal discipline, in other words, is not an obstacle standing in the way of growth; it is the foundation the private sector needs in order to build durable capital.

Spain's membership in the euro sharpens this picture further, because it removes one of the traditional channels of adjustment. Inside a monetary union, a country cannot devalue its way back to competitiveness; the burden of adjustment must instead fall on internal prices and wages. This is where the interaction between the business cycle and labor market regulation becomes decisive. The structural unemployment that persists even in good times — the 2.42 million people still out of work in November 2025 — is the product of a volatile cycle acting on a rigid regulatory framework. The cycle sets the direction of the tide; rigid labor law determines the severity of the flood. In a labor-abundant economy, a negative shock calls for the price of labor to adjust downward in order to preserve jobs. But severance costs and inflexible collective bargaining block that price adjustment, so the market clears instead through quantity — mass layoffs rather than a temporary trim in wages or hours. Liberalization will not abolish the business cycle; nothing does. But it is the shock absorber Spain currently lacks. If this period of growth is not used to loosen those rigidities, the next downturn will once again be paid for in destroyed jobs rather than in a manageable adjustment.

None of this is a reason to dismiss the November figures. They are a genuine, welcome respite — a market that keeps breathing even in the seasonal cold. But treating them as proof that Spanish economic management has been fixed is a category error. A sharp rise in employment is exactly what a labor-abundant economy produces on the way up, just as sharp falls are what it produces on the way down; neither, by itself, tells us anything about the durability of the underlying model. The route to a genuinely resilient Spain does not run through celebrating cyclical highs, nor through fiscal stimulus that mortgages future solvency. It runs through an environment that rewards capital accumulation over debt, and a labor market freed from the rigidities that amplify every turn of the cycle. Until sound money, balanced budgets, and structural flexibility are treated as the priority rather than the afterthought, Spain will keep riding the same rollercoaster — with its workers absorbing the cost of every drop.

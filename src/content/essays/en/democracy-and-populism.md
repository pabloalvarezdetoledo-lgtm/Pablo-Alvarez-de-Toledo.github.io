Representative democracy enjoys, in contemporary public discourse, an almost unquestionable status: it is presented as the natural culmination of political progress, the horizon against which every alternative looks like regression. That reverence, however, makes it harder to see its structural limitations clearly - limitations that are not malfunctions but features built into its own architecture. This essay sets out to examine those limitations, to understand why populism emerges as a response - at once understandable and dangerous - to them, and to explore whether an idea as rarely visited as acracy, a system without centralised authority, can offer some element of balance between the two.

The first limitation of representative democracy is that it concentrates power in the hands of a few, even though its legitimacy rests precisely on the opposite promise. Representatives are supposed to act on behalf of citizens; in practice, however, interest groups and established political elites exert disproportionate influence. Corporations, unions and lobbying organisations fund campaigns, help draft legislation and enjoy access to decision-making that the ordinary citizen does not have. Added to this is a political class that tends to perpetuate itself: careers stretch across decades, power concentrates among familiar names, and the renewal and diversity of decision-making suffer as a result. The outcome is a democracy formally open to everyone but materially captured by a few, whose policies reflect the interests of those with resources more faithfully than the needs of those without them.

The second limitation is the influence of money in politics, which compounds the first and gives it concrete economic shape. The rising cost of election campaigns forces candidates to depend on donations and financial contributions, granting those who make them a capacity for pressure wholly disproportionate to their demographic weight. The absence of effective regulation over political financing, and the lack of transparency around donations, are not minor flaws in the system but conditions that entrench inequality of opportunity - both among those competing for power and, ultimately, among the interests that power ends up serving.

The third limitation, perhaps the deepest, is the real poverty of citizen participation. Democracy is rhetorically justified as government of the people, but in practice that participation is reduced, for the vast majority of citizens, to voting once every few years. The drafting of laws, the design of public policy, and the decisions that truly matter remain in the hands of elected representatives and the elites around them, while the direct voice of citizens is excluded from the process except in its most sporadic and most simplified form: the ballot. Out of this disconnect between citizens and the processes that decide over their lives grows a discontent that is not irrational, but perfectly coherent with the lived experience of a system that promises popular sovereignty and delivers, in practice, administration by permanent delegation.

It is precisely in this terrain that populism finds its reason for being. Following Ernesto Laclau's reading, populism can be understood not as the pathology of a charismatic leader nor as the vice of a particular party, but as a discursive construction that articulates scattered demands and accumulated frustrations around a collective identity: "the people." Seen in this light, populism is not an anomaly that erupts from outside the democratic system, but a response born from its own cracks. Its central criticism of democracy's limited representativeness is, to a considerable extent, accurate: it gives voice to excluded sectors, denounces the capture of power by economic and political elites, and presents populist leaders as defenders of a majority that perceives itself as distant from a ruling class indifferent to its real needs. In its demand for more direct mechanisms of participation - consultations, referendums, spaces for deliberation - populism rightly attempts to overcome the passivity to which representative democracy condemns the citizen between one election and the next.

But acknowledging the legitimacy of the populist diagnosis is not the same as accepting its solutions, because populism carries its own dangers, no less serious than those it denounces. The first is the oversimplification of complex problems. Populist leaders tend to offer easy explanations for structural challenges, identifying a common enemy - the elite, the foreigner, financial power - to which responsibilities are attributed that rarely admit a single cause. This oversimplification produces a view of the world divided between "us" and "them," in which "the people" is presented as a homogeneous entity that excludes, almost by definition, whoever does not fit its majoritarian narrative.

The second danger is emotional manipulation. Populist discourse appeals systematically to emotion and fear rather than to reasoned deliberation, seeking a direct connection with its audience that bypasses analysis in favour of belonging. That appeal can generate deep divisions, feed intolerance toward those perceived as adversaries, and weaken the social fabric precisely at the moment when building consensus is most needed.

The third danger, and perhaps the gravest, is authoritarian drift. In its eagerness to represent the will of the majority, populist leadership tends to erode institutional checks and balances, concentrating power and weakening the separation of powers that exists precisely to protect against the very abuse it claims to be fighting. This tendency can translate into restrictions on press freedom, the weakening of judicial independence, and an active disregard for the rights of minorities - immigrants, ethnic, religious or sexual minorities - who fall outside the majoritarian definition of the people. This disrespect for diversity contradicts the liberal principle that every citizen, whether or not they belong to the majority, deserves equal rights and equal treatment.

The fourth danger is short-termism. By focusing on the immediate satisfaction of popular demands, populism tends to neglect the longer-horizon policies that structural challenges require, favouring impulsive decisions whose economic and social consequences only become apparent later. The expectations it generates are frequently unrealistic, and their disappointment feeds a disillusion that further erodes trust in the political system, closing a self-reinforcing loop: the distrust that produced populism ends up reinforced by populism itself.

We find ourselves, then, facing two systems that criticise each other with valid reasons and that, at the same time, generate their own problems: a representative democracy captured by elites and disconnected from its citizens, and a populism that denounces this capture but threatens to replace deliberation with oversimplification, and pluralism with the forced homogeneity of an imagined "people." Faced with this apparent impasse, it is worth exploring a third possibility, as rarely travelled as it is suggestive: acracy.

Acracy designates a system in which power is not concentrated in any centralised authority but is distributed broadly and in decentralised fashion, favouring the direct participation of citizens in decision-making and horizontal collaboration in building solutions. Against representative democracy, acracy promises to overcome the permanent delegation of power to a few; against populism, it promises to avoid concentrating that same power in a leader or in a homogenising majoritarian narrative. In principle, it would offer the best of both worlds: the direct participation that populism demands, without the risk of that participation crystallising around an authoritarian figure or an exclusionary identity.

It would, however, be naïve to present acracy as a panacea. Its implementation requires solid structures and mechanisms capable of facilitating citizen participation without opening the door to manipulation or to domination concealed by organised groups, which, in the absence of any central authority, might find new - and perhaps less visible - ways of capturing the process. Direct participation, moreover, does not by itself guarantee better decisions: it can lead to a proliferation of polarised opinions, a lack of consensus, and slow, inefficient decision-making - precisely the defects already levelled against representative democracy when it confronts complex problems. And perhaps the most demanding condition of all: an acratic system requires a high level of civic education and informed participation, without which the decentralisation of power produces not genuine deliberation but simply a dispersion of the problem.

The conclusion of this inquiry, then, is not the replacement of one system by another, but the recognition that none of the three - representative democracy, populism, acracy - resolves on its own the tension between representativeness, participation and stability. The most reasonable approach is a balanced one that combines the institutional elements of representative democracy, capable of protecting rights and freedoms against the abuse of any majority; the critical energy of populism, capable of questioning the status quo and the concentrations of power that formal democracy tends to naturalise; and the spaces of direct and deliberative participation that acracy calls for, provided they rest on solid structures and on a citizenry formed to exercise them responsibly. None of these three elements suffices on its own. Together, however imperfectly, they offer the possibility of a political society that is more just, more inclusive, and better able to sustain itself over time.

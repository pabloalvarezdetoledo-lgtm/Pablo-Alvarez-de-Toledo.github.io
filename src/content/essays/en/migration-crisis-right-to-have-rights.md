In chapter IX of *The Origins of Totalitarianism* — "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man" — Hannah Arendt turns to a figure the liberal tradition prefers not to look at directly: the stateless person. She did not arrive at that figure by a purely theoretical route. She had fled Nazi Germany, had watched thousands of Jews and members of other minorities lose their nationality before they lost their lives, and had learned firsthand that ceasing to belong to a state is not the loss of one administrative benefit among others, but the loss of the very ground from which any right can be claimed. Out of that experience comes one of the most uncomfortable theses in twentieth-century political philosophy: human rights, presented as inherent to the person simply by virtue of being human, in practice depend almost entirely on membership in a particular political community.

Arendt's diagnosis begins with a precise definition of the nation-state: a political form in which the nation — understood as a shared cultural, linguistic or historical identity — becomes the criterion that decides who is a citizen and who is not, and therefore who may invoke political and civil rights and who is left outside that possibility. As long as a person remains inside that community, the system works: the right exists because some institution stands ready to recognize it, enforce it, answer for it. But the moment someone loses their nationality — through persecution, through war, through the dissolution of a state, or through the simple administrative decision of another state to withhold recognition — they discover that behind their supposedly "inalienable" rights there was no authority capable of guaranteeing them. The stateless person is not a citizen with fewer rights; they are, in practice, someone to whom no right can be applied, because no one is obligated to apply it. It is this paradox that leads Arendt to her most cited formulation: prior to any particular right, there exists a "right to have rights" — the right to belong to a political community willing to guarantee everything else. Without that prior right, all other rights are not inalienable; they are simply inapplicable.

Arendt's proposed solution is at once modest and radical: a "republic of human rights," a political organization capable of protecting and guaranteeing rights independently of the nationality of whoever claims them. Modest, because it does not aim to abolish the state or the idea of the nation; radical, because it demands separating what the nineteenth century fused together — membership in a right should not depend on membership in a nation.

This is where it is worth pushing a step beyond Arendt. Her diagnosis attributes the decline of the nation-state mainly to globalization and to growing economic interdependence among countries. But, in my view, the problem is not only that the nation-state is losing ground to external forces; the problem is structural and prior to that: since the nineteenth century, "state" and "nation" have been understood as a single concept, as though every nation needed its own state and every state had to correspond to a nation. Nations, however, are dynamic entities — communities bound by shared language, religion, history or memory — that arise, change, and sometimes disappear according to an enormous variety of factors; they are not, and cannot be, fixed or eternal units. Fusing that changing reality with the fixed, sovereign machinery of the state has one very concrete consequence: the state inherits from the nation the power to decide, unilaterally, who belongs to the political community and who does not. Majorities and minorities then appear within its borders, and with them the possibility — too often realized — of the oppression of some by others. The stateless person is the most extreme consequence of that design: someone whom no nation-state recognizes as part of "its" nation, and whom, therefore, no state feels obliged to protect.

Faced with the question of whether stateless people and refugees should be admitted into existing political communities, it is worth not caricaturing the arguments raised against doing so, because several of them are understandable and deserve an answer rather than contempt. There is, first, the security argument: the idea that among those who arrive without papers or verifiable background there could be criminals, hostile agents or dangerous individuals. The argument is not without logic, though it is worth remembering that the overwhelming majority of stateless people and refugees are, precisely, victims — people fleeing persecution, violence or oppression, not those who inflict it. There is, second, the integration argument: the real difficulty of learning a language, finding work or adapting to unfamiliar customs, and the cost that process imposes on host societies. This argument, too, has some basis, though integration, properly understood, is not a burden that falls on the newcomer alone but a mutual process requiring investment and goodwill from both sides. There is, third, the cultural argument: the fear, not always unfounded, that coexistence between communities with a history of conflict — I think of the confrontation between Israel and Palestine, or of the tensions that the arrival of communities seeking to govern themselves by their own rules has generated in some European neighborhoods — will prove harder than goodwill tends to assume. It is an understandable fear, rooted in a well-documented human tendency to glorify what is one's own and distrust what is unfamiliar — but a fear that, examined closely, is an argument for dialogue and time, not for permanent exclusion.

Of all these arguments, however, the one that deserves to be taken most seriously is not the cultural one but the fiscal one. Since the middle of the last century, Western democracies have built a welfare-state model that requires, to sustain itself, a considerable tax burden. There is no need to settle here whether that model is desirable, or whether better alternatives exist, in order to recognize an arithmetical fact: granting full citizenship, indiscriminately, to millions of stateless people would create a sudden demand for education, healthcare, housing and social services that no welfare system is designed to absorb all at once. The response to that overspending would inevitably be higher taxes or higher public debt, and either path generates discontent that, far from being distributed equally, tends to concentrate among those who have the least and depend the most on those very services. This is not an argument invented to justify rejection: it is a genuine practical constraint, and any serious proposal about statelessness has to reckon with it rather than pretend it does not exist.

Recognizing that the fiscal argument is real, however, is not the same as accepting that it is the reason actually driving states to keep stateless people excluded. My own sense is that states cultivate and amplify hostility toward the foreigner — that almost instinctive tribalism mentioned above — precisely because it is more comfortable than confronting the structural question: what, institutionally, to do with those who do not fit the nation-state equation inherited from the nineteenth century. Instrumentalized, xenophobia functions as an excuse: it lets a state present as spontaneous popular rejection what is really the comfort of not having to redesign its own institutions. This does not invalidate the real fiscal constraint behind it; it simply warns that this constraint, legitimate in itself, is often used as cover for avoiding the deeper problem.

That is why I think Arendt's most valuable contribution is not so much the "republic of human rights" — a proposal that, as she herself acknowledged, requires an institutional architecture that does not yet exist — as the diagnosis that precedes it: as long as nation and state continue to be understood as one and the same thing, the state will retain the power to decide, on its own, who counts as a bearer of rights and who does not. Stateless people are not a marginal anomaly of the international system; they are proof that the system, as currently built, necessarily produces the excluded. Any honest solution has to start from that observation, hold without cynicism to the real fiscal constraint the welfare state imposes, and refuse, at the same time, to let that constraint serve as cover for avoiding a rethinking of the relation among nation, state and rights.

abstract: "This thesis asks whether ECB and Eurosystem liquidity expansions alter the relative valuation of assets with different financial durations, using the return spread between the MSCI Europe Growth and MSCI Europe Value indices as an imperfect proxy for duration. Using a hand-built monthly dataset from January 2000 to February 2026, it estimates a Eurosystem liquidity reaction function to isolate residual liquidity innovations from the central bank's endogenous response to financial conditions, then traces their dynamic effects with local projections and Newey-West HAC standard errors. Liquidity is found to respond partly to prior financial stress, and residual innovations do not produce a uniform improvement in aggregate financial conditions - the CISS stress indicator rises at some horizons and the EURO STOXX 50's cumulative response is negative - while evidence of regime dependence is not robust. In contrast, the Growth-minus-Value spread responds positively and reaches pointwise statistical significance at short and medium horizons, a pattern consistent with, though not structural proof of, a financial-duration channel."
researchQuestion: "Do the ECB's and the Eurosystem's liquidity expansion measures favour the relative appreciation of longer-duration assets, approximated by Growth portfolios, over shorter-duration assets, approximated by Value portfolios? This central question is broken into four sub-questions: whether Eurosystem liquidity reacts endogenously to financial stress; whether residual liquidity innovations are associated with dynamic changes in financial conditions (systemic stress, volatility, aggregate returns, the term structure); whether transmission is regime-dependent, i.e. stronger during episodes of stress than in normal times; and whether the response of the Growth-minus-Value spread is consistent with the financial-duration hypothesis."
methodology: "The empirical strategy has two steps. First, a Eurosystem liquidity reaction function regresses changes in the Eurosystem's balance sheet (with excess liquidity as a complementary measure) on its own lags and on financial-conditions variables (the CISS systemic-stress indicator, VSTOXX implied volatility, aggregate stock returns, the deposit facility rate and the yield curve) to separate the central bank's systematic response from a residual liquidity innovation. Second, that standardised residual innovation is fed into local projections - horizon-by-horizon regressions with Newey-West HAC standard errors - to estimate the cumulative dynamic response of financial-stress indicators, the EURO STOXX 50, and the Growth-minus-Value return differential, plus a regime-dependent specification testing whether responses differ between high-stress and normal periods (defined by CISS quantile thresholds). The dataset is a monthly panel (January 2000-February 2026, implemented in Python) combining Eurosystem balance-sheet data, CISS, VSTOXX, euro-area sovereign yield curves, the EURO STOXX 50, and the MSCI Europe Growth and Value indices."
findings: "Eurosystem liquidity is confirmed to respond partly to its own past values and to prior financial-stress conditions, supporting the view that it is not exogenous. Residual liquidity innovations do not translate into a uniform improvement in aggregate financial conditions: the CISS indicator rises at some horizons and the cumulative EURO STOXX 50 response is negative, while evidence of stronger transmission during stress regimes is not robust. The clearest result concerns the Growth-minus-Value spread: positive residual liquidity innovations are followed by a statistically significant relative outperformance of Growth over Value at short-to-medium horizons, peaking at h = 5 months in the baseline specification (coefficient approximately 0.0092, p = 0.006) and at h = 4 months in an alternative balance-sheet-growth specification (coefficient approximately 0.0101, p = 0.0022), with the qualitative sign stable across robustness checks. The thesis concludes this is consistent with, but does not structurally identify, a financial-duration channel through which European monetary easing disproportionately benefits longer-duration (Growth) assets - a reading qualified by the pan-European (rather than strictly euro-area) scope of the Growth/Value proxy."

## About this entry

This page presents Pablo's final degree thesis (Trabajo de Fin de Grado) in Economics and International Business, submitted at Universidad Antonio de Nebrija (Madrid) in June 2026 under the supervision of Raquel García-Donas Guerrero. It sits at the centre of the site's research programme: it asks whether the terms on which the ECB and the Eurosystem supply liquidity to the financial system feed through not just to the overall level of asset prices, but to their relative pricing across different financial durations — from short-dated, cash-flow-near assets to long-duration equities such as Growth stocks.

Empirically, the thesis builds a monthly dataset (January 2000–February 2026) combining Eurosystem balance-sheet data, systemic-stress and volatility indicators, the euro-area term structure of interest rates, the EURO STOXX 50, and the MSCI Europe Growth and Value indices. It estimates a liquidity reaction function to separate the Eurosystem's endogenous response to financial stress from residual liquidity innovations, then uses local projections to trace how those innovations affect financial conditions and, in particular, the Growth-minus-Value return spread over time.

abstract: "This paper examines a question prior — both logically and conceptually — to the ordinary debate over veganism and animal rights: what makes an entity an original subject of fundamental rights. From that distinction the paper develops a non-functional theory of the person, on which personhood does not depend on the current exercise of intelligence, autonomy, memory or accountability, but on a personal nature whose mature form of operation includes reflective rationality, diachronic identity, the capacity to adopt and revise ends, and participation in normative practices of justification, demand and responsibility. ## About this entry

This working paper asks a question prior to the ordinary debate over animal rights and veganism: what makes an entity an original subject of fundamental rights — a status the legal order can specify or protect, but does not itself constitute. The paper separates legal personality, an institutional attribution that can be granted, withheld or fragmented for functional purposes, from ontological personhood, a mode of being that does not depend on an act of state recognition. It then defends a non-functional account of personhood: what makes someone a person is not the current exercise of intelligence, autonomy, memory or accountability, but belonging to a personal nature whose mature form of life includes reflective rationality, a temporally extended identity, and participation in practices of justification and responsibility — a view meant to explain why infants, the sleeping and the cognitively disabled retain full personal status without exception.

From this account of the person, the paper derives an account of dignity as a non-gradable status rather than a magnitude that tracks cognitive performance, and from dignity it derives a category of "status rights" — fundamental rights grounded not in institutional grant or aggregable utility, but in a form of value that limits how a bearer may be instrumentalised. It then turns this framework on the status of animals, conceding fully the contemporary scientific case for animal sentience and consciousness, but arguing that sentience alone — without a further normative premise — does not entail personhood or a right to life of the same structure as a human being's. Engaging directly with Singer, Regan, Korsgaard, Francione, Donaldson and Kymlicka, and Nussbaum, and with the argument from marginal cases and the charge of speciesism, the paper concludes that killing an animal is not conceptually equivalent to homicide and that consuming animal products is not, by its bare structure, intrinsically immoral — while leaving fully open a demanding moral scrutiny of cruelty, unnecessary suffering, environmental harm and breaches of duties of care in any given case.
